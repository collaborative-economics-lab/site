America has a forever war problem — but the diagnosis is wrong.

For two decades, critics of U.S. foreign policy have correctly identified the human cost of perpetual engagement: the body bags, the trillion-dollar price tags, the moral erosion of sending a professional soldier class to cycle endlessly through Kandahar and Fallujah while the rest of the country watches from their couches. The lesson the public drew was: *forever wars are unacceptable.*

They were right. But they drew the wrong conclusion.

The problem was never the *war*. The problem was the *soldiers*.

We are entering what I call the **Age of Andral** — a new strategic and economic paradigm in which the United States projects power not through human bodies, but through autonomous machines. In this paradigm, the forever war isn't a tragedy. It's a growth engine.

<!--more-->

## What Is the Age of Andral?

The term *andral* captures the essential quality of this new era: the replacement of human agency in conflict with machine autonomy. Just as the industrial age replaced human muscle with mechanical force, the andral age replaces human risk with algorithmic decision-making on the battlefield.

This isn't science fiction. It's already underway:

- **Lethal Autonomous Weapons (LAWs)** — drones, ground vehicles, and undersea systems capable of identifying and engaging targets without a human in the loop — are being actively developed by the U.S., Russia, China, Israel, and South Korea.
- The U.S. **Phalanx CIWS** has been autonomously shooting down incoming missiles since the 1970s. Israel's **Iron Dome** autonomously intercepts rockets. South Korea deploys autonomous sentry guns along the DMZ.
- DARPA's autonomous drone programs, the U.S. Navy's **Ghost Fleet** concept, and the Army's **Robotic Combat Vehicle** program represent the leading edge of a transition already well underway.

The andral age isn't a prediction. It's a description of where we already are.

## The Economics: rGDP and the Expenditure Engine

Here's the thesis in clean economic terms:

**Defense spending on autonomous systems is a net rGDP contributor that imposes asymmetric costs on adversaries.**

### Domestic rGDP Effects

Real GDP measures the inflation-adjusted value of all goods and services produced in an economy. Defense spending enters through the government expenditure component: `GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)`.

When the U.S. deploys autonomous combat systems that actually *operate* continuously — consuming fuel, maintenance, replacement parts, software updates, and communications infrastructure — the expenditure is ongoing. A robot war has a fundamentally different economic profile than a human war:

| Factor | Human War | Robot War |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Primary cost | Recruitment, training, healthcare, casualties | Hardware, software, logistics, replacement |
| Domestic multiplier | Moderate | High (manufacturing, R&D stay domestic) |
| Duration tolerance | Low (public opposition, human cost) | High (no flag-draped coffins) |
| Innovation spillover | Low | High (AI, robotics, materials science) |
| Post-conflict cost | Enormous (VA, disability, PTSD) | Near-zero (scrap the unit) |

The robot war generates *more domestic economic activity per dollar spent* and is *cheaper per year of engagement*. Consider the innovation spillover alone: GPS came from the military. The internet came from ARPA. Autonomous vehicles, computer vision, precision agriculture — all descendants of military robotics investment.

### The Asymmetric Adversary Tax

When the United States deploys robot armies, adversaries face a brutal choice: **match our capability or accept strategic irrelevance.**

Matching requires massive domestic investment in AI, robotics, and autonomous systems — capital diverted from civilian economic development, continuous technological catch-up as we advance. This is the **adversary tax**.

It compounds asymmetrically because the U.S. has structural advantages no other nation currently matches:

- **Silicon Valley + Pentagon pipeline**: The world's deepest private AI and robotics ecosystem. Companies like Anduril and Shield AI represent private capital flowing *toward* defense autonomy. Adversaries finance it entirely through state budgets.
- **Capital markets**: We finance defense R&D through public contracts *and* private venture capital.
- **Dollar hegemony**: U.S. defense spending is denominated in dollars. Adversary defense spending must ultimately reach dollar-denominated semiconductor supply chains — predominantly manufactured in U.S.-aligned economies.

For every dollar the U.S. spends on autonomous warfare, adversaries must spend *more* to stay competitive — directly at the expense of their civilian economies.

## The Political Economy: Zero Casket Tolerance

The deepest problem with human-based forever wars is political. Democracies have low tolerance for casualties. That tolerance ended Vietnam. It eroded support for Iraq and Afghanistan. "Bring the troops home" became a bipartisan rallying cry.

Robot wars break this constraint entirely.

When an autonomous combat drone is destroyed, there is no next-of-kin notification. No Gold Star family. No congressional inquiry. There is a procurement order for a replacement unit.

This transforms the political economy of sustained engagement. The United States can sustain autonomous military engagement *indefinitely* without the political cost that has historically ended American wars. That's not a bug. It's the core feature.

## Objections Worth Taking Seriously

**"Won't adversaries develop autonomous weapons too?"**

Yes — eventually. But the race itself is the point. Every year spent in the development gap is a year the adversary diverts economic resources from civilian growth to military catch-up. The U.S. advantage in AI — anchored in private sector investment that no state-directed economy can easily replicate — suggests this gap is structural.

**"Doesn't this lower the barrier to war?"**

This is the most serious objection. The traditional friction against war includes human cost. Remove it and you remove a deterrent.

The counterargument: the alternative to robot-fought proxy conflicts isn't peace — it's human-fought proxy conflicts. And a deterrent shifts: if engaging U.S. interests means facing an autonomous military system you cannot match and cannot politically exhaust, that's powerful disincentive in itself.

**"What about international law?"**

A legitimate concern. Laws of armed conflict require distinction, proportionality, and command accountability. Autonomous systems complicate all three. But this is a governance problem, not an argument against the technology. The same questions were asked about airpower, nuclear weapons, and drones. International norms lag technology. That's historically normal.

## The Collaborative Economics Lens

Through the lens of collaborative economics, the andral paradigm is genuinely complex.

On one hand, autonomous warfare concentrates benefits (GDP growth, technological spillover, strategic dominance) in the United States while exporting costs (forced military spending, diverted development resources) to adversaries. That's extraction at geopolitical scale.

On the other hand, it substitutes machines for human lives — including the lives of working-class Americans who have historically borne the human cost of American wars. If those lives have been spent cheaply and unjustly in the service of abstract strategic interests, then a paradigm that removes human bodies from the equation deserves serious consideration.

The andral age doesn't resolve whether American military engagement serves human flourishing. What it does is change the terms — and in doing so, opens a new conversation about what war costs, who bears those costs, and whether the old moral calculus still applies.

---

## Conclusion

The age of andral is not a choice. It's arriving regardless — driven by AI progress, by great power competition, by the structural inability of democratic societies to sustain human casualties in discretionary conflicts.

A strategically managed andral paradigm: sustained investment in autonomous systems that keeps adversaries perpetually behind, generates continuous domestic rGDP through defense-industrial activity, produces technological spillovers compounding civilian economic advantages — without the political and human costs that have historically constrained American power.

Is it morally comfortable? No. Strategic advantage rarely is.

Is it economically coherent? Demonstrably yes.

The forever war powered by humans was unacceptable. The forever war powered by robots is a different calculation entirely — and America's rivals are already doing that math.

We should be doing it better.
