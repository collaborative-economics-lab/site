## Introduction: The Forever War Problem

America has a forever war problem — but the diagnosis is wrong.

For two decades, critics of U.S. foreign policy have correctly identified the human cost of perpetual engagement: the body bags, the trillion-dollar price tags, the moral erosion of sending a professional soldier class to cycle endlessly through Kandahar and Fallujah while the rest of the country watches from their couches. Vietnam broke the draft. Iraq and Afghanistan broke the professional military. The lesson the public drew was: *forever wars are unacceptable.*

They were right. But they drew the wrong conclusion.

The problem was never the *war*. The problem was the *soldiers*.

<!--more-->

We are entering what I call the **Age of Anduril** — a new strategic and economic paradigm in which the United States projects power not through human bodies, but through autonomous machines. The name comes from Anduril Industries: the defense technology company founded by Palmer Luckey in 2017 that is building the infrastructure for this new kind of war. In Tolkien's Quenya, *Andúril* means "Flame of the West." In American strategic terms, it means something more prosaic and more significant: the replacement of human risk with algorithmic decision-making on the battlefield.

In this paradigm, the forever war isn't a tragedy. It's a growth engine.

## What Is Anduril, and Why Does It Matter?

Anduril Industries is not a traditional defense contractor. It's a software-first, Silicon Valley-born company that builds autonomous systems — drones, surveillance towers, counter-drone platforms, undersea vehicles, and the AI that ties them together — and sells them to the U.S. Department of Defense.

The numbers are clarifying:
- **Founded**: 2017
- **Revenue**: $2 billion (2025)
- **Employees**: 7,000 (2026)
- **Current flagship project**: Arsenal-1, a hyperscale computing and weapons manufacturing facility being built near Columbus, Ohio in partnership with Ohio State University — designed to produce advanced autonomous weapons faster than near-peer rivals

Anduril's products include:
- **Autonomous surveillance systems** (Sentry Towers) deployed on the U.S.-Mexico border and military installations globally
- **Unmanned aerial systems (UAS)** and counter-UAS (CUAS) platforms
- **Lattice OS** — an AI-powered command and control network that connects autonomous assets in real time
- **Dive-LD** — long-range autonomous undersea vehicles
- **Fury** — an autonomous fighter jet designed to operate in contested airspace without a human pilot

Anduril is not alone. Shield AI, Palantir (Project Maven), General Atomics, and dozens of VC-backed defense startups are building the same future. But Anduril is the clearest articulation of the thesis: **America's next military advantage is autonomous systems built like software products, not defense procurement bureaucracies.**

## The Economic Case: rGDP and the Expenditure Engine

Here's the thesis in clean economic terms:

**Defense spending on autonomous systems is a net rGDP contributor that imposes asymmetric costs on adversaries.**

### Half 1: Domestic rGDP Effects

Real GDP measures the inflation-adjusted value of all goods and services produced in an economy. Defense spending enters GDP through the government expenditure component: `GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)`, where `G` is government spending.

The critical distinction between a human war and a robot war is what the spending *buys* and how long it *lasts*.

A human war's primary costs are personnel: recruitment, training, salaries, healthcare, combat pay, and — most expensively — post-war obligations. Veterans' benefits, disability claims, PTSD treatment, and long-term healthcare for combat injuries cost the U.S. economy an estimated **$2.2 trillion** in post-9/11 veteran care alone (Brown University Costs of War Project). Much of this spending flows *out* of the productive economy and into consumption by individuals who can no longer contribute economically at full capacity.

A robot war's costs are industrial: hardware, software, logistics, maintenance, replacement units, and the R&D pipeline that keeps the systems competitive. These costs:

1. **Stay in the domestic economy** — Anduril's Arsenal-1 factory is in Ohio. The engineers writing Lattice OS are in California. The supply chain is in U.S.-aligned semiconductor fabs.
2. **Generate innovation spillover** — GPS, the internet, and computer vision all emerged from military R&D. Autonomous systems are doing the same for AI, robotics, and materials science.
3. **Are continuous** — a deployed autonomous system consumes fuel, bandwidth, parts, and software updates indefinitely. That's ongoing GDP contribution, not a one-time purchase.
4. **Have near-zero post-conflict costs** — when an autonomous drone is destroyed or obsolete, you order a new one. There's no VA claim, no disability pension, no lifetime healthcare obligation.

| Factor | Human War | Robot War |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Primary cost | Personnel, training, casualties | Hardware, software, R&D |
| Post-conflict cost | Enormous (VA, disability, PTSD) | Near-zero |
| Domestic multiplier | Moderate | High (manufacturing + R&D stay domestic) |
| Duration tolerance | Low (public opposition) | High (no casualties) |
| Innovation spillover | Low | High (AI, robotics, materials) |
| Replacement cost | High (years to train a soldier) | Low (manufacture another unit) |

### Half 2: The Asymmetric Adversary Tax

This is where the strategic economics get genuinely interesting.

When the United States deploys autonomous systems at scale, adversaries face a binary choice: **match our capability or accept strategic irrelevance.**

Matching requires:
1. Massive domestic investment in AI, robotics, and autonomous weapons production
2. Diversion of capital from civilian economic development
3. Continuous technological catch-up as the U.S. advances

This is the **adversary tax** — the forced expenditure that every peer competitor must make simply to remain relevant on the battlefield. And it compounds asymmetrically.

Why asymmetrically? Because the United States has structural advantages that no other nation currently matches:

**1. The Silicon Valley-Pentagon Pipeline**

Anduril was co-founded by Palmer Luckey (creator of the Oculus Rift) and Trae Stephens (Founders Fund, ex-Palantir). The company raised venture capital from the same investors who backed Facebook and SpaceX. This represents a fundamental shift: America's most talented engineers and its most aggressive private capital are now flowing *toward* defense autonomy, not away from it.

China's tech companies operate under party direction and cannot independently allocate capital toward autonomous weapons development at Silicon Valley's velocity. Russia's defense sector is chronically underfunded, corruption-riddled, and dependent on aging Soviet-era industrial infrastructure. Neither can replicate the feedback loop between private AI development and military application that the U.S. now has.

**2. Capital Markets Advantage**

The U.S. finances defense R&D through a combination of DoD contracts *and* private venture capital. Anduril has raised over $4.6 billion in private funding — that's risk capital that doesn't appear on the federal budget, doesn't require congressional appropriation, and moves at startup speed.

Adversaries finance autonomous weapons development entirely through state budget allocation. Every dollar spent on robot armies is a dollar not spent on hospitals, roads, schools, or consumer goods — the civilian outputs that sustain public legitimacy in both democracies and authoritarian systems.

**3. Dollar Hegemony and Semiconductor Control**

Advanced autonomous systems require advanced semiconductors. The global semiconductor supply chain is overwhelmingly concentrated in U.S.-aligned economies: TSMC (Taiwan), Samsung (South Korea), ASML (Netherlands), and U.S. fabs. Export controls on chips to China and Russia aren't just economic policy — they're a structural constraint on adversary autonomous weapons development.

U.S. defense spending is denominated in dollars, which the U.S. issues. Adversary defense spending must ultimately reach dollar-denominated inputs. That asymmetry compounds over time.

**The result**: For every dollar the U.S. spends on autonomous warfare capacity, adversaries must spend *more* to stay competitive — and that spending comes at the direct expense of civilian economic output. The adversary tax is real, ongoing, and self-reinforcing.

## The Political Economy: Zero Casket Tolerance

The deepest problem with human-based forever wars isn't economic. It's political.

Democracies have low tolerance for casualties. That tolerance ended Vietnam. It eroded support for Iraq and Afghanistan over a decade of mounting losses. "Bring the troops home" became a bipartisan rallying cry precisely because American families were receiving flag-draped coffins.

Autonomous warfare breaks this political constraint entirely.

When an Anduril Fury autonomous fighter is shot down over contested airspace, the Pentagon issues no next-of-kin notification. There is no Gold Star family. There is no congressional inquiry into why a young American died for abstract strategic interests. There is a procurement order for a replacement unit.

This transforms the political economy of sustained engagement. The public opposition that ended Vietnam, that eroded Iraq and Afghanistan — none of it applies when the combatants are machines. The United States can sustain autonomous military presence *indefinitely* without the political cost that has historically ended American military engagements.

That's not a cynical observation. It's a structural fact with profound strategic implications.

## The Competitive Dynamic: Forcing Adversaries to Play Our Game

The most underappreciated dimension of autonomous warfare economics is how it *restructures global competition*.

When the U.S. commits to an autonomous warfare paradigm, it doesn't just create domestic GDP growth — it forces adversaries into a competition that favors U.S. structural advantages.

Consider the dynamics:

**China** must respond to U.S. autonomous weapons development by investing in its own program. This means:
- Redirecting engineering talent from civilian AI (where it competes commercially with U.S. firms) to military applications
- Increasing defense budget allocation, which reduces available capital for Belt and Road infrastructure investment and domestic social spending
- Accelerating the confrontation between party control and the technical freedom that innovation requires

**Russia** faces even starker constraints:
- A GDP roughly the size of Texas means every autonomous weapons dollar crowds out something else
- An already-strained defense industrial base revealed by Ukraine performance
- Dependence on imports for advanced semiconductors, now heavily sanctioned

**Every other potential adversary** faces the same equation: match or fall behind, and matching costs more than it costs us.

This is the strategic logic of the Anduril age: not just that we win, but that the competition itself is more expensive for everyone else. That's a sustained drag on adversary GDP growth, a permanent R&D tax that we don't pay but they do.

## Objections Worth Taking Seriously

### "Doesn't this lower the barrier to war, making conflict more frequent?"

This is the most serious objection. Traditional deterrence theory holds that the human cost of war creates friction against starting one. Remove that cost and you remove a significant deterrent.

The counterargument has two parts. First, the alternative to robot-fought proxy conflicts isn't peace — it's human-fought proxy conflicts. The U.S. has been involved in proxy conflicts continuously since World War II regardless of the human cost calculus. Second, deterrence shifts: if an adversary knows that engaging U.S. interests means facing an autonomous military system it cannot match, cannot politically exhaust, and cannot win an attrition war against, that's a powerful disincentive. The nature of deterrence changes, but deterrence doesn't disappear.

### "Won't adversaries develop autonomous weapons too, eventually neutralizing the advantage?"

Yes — eventually. But the race itself is the point. The development gap is years of adversary economic resources diverted from civilian growth to military catch-up. Moreover, the U.S. structural advantage in AI — anchored in private sector investment, talent concentration, and semiconductor access — suggests the gap is not temporary. It may widen.

### "What about international law and accountability?"

Legitimate concern. Laws of armed conflict require distinction between combatants and civilians, proportionality, and command accountability. Autonomous systems complicate all three. The "human in the loop" question — who is responsible when an autonomous weapon kills a civilian — remains unresolved in international law.

This is a governance problem that needs solving. But it's not an argument against the technology — it's an argument for developing legal frameworks in parallel with the technology. The same questions were asked about strategic bombing, about nuclear weapons, about armed drones. International norms lag technology. That's historically normal, not a disqualifying problem.

### "Is this just the military-industrial complex rebranded?"

Eisenhower warned in 1961 about "the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." That warning was about the political capture of defense spending — contracts flowing to legacy primes regardless of merit, sustained by lobbying and congressional district politics.

Anduril and the new defense tech ecosystem are, in some ways, a correction to that problem — not its continuation. Luckey's explicit thesis is that the legacy defense primes (Lockheed, Raytheon, Boeing) are too slow, too bureaucratic, and too expensive. The new paradigm is software-first, outcome-oriented, and competitively priced. Whether that holds as these companies scale is a legitimate question. But the critique of the MIC applies less cleanly to the new cohort than to the old.

## The Collaborative Economics Lens

This blog concerns itself with collaborative economics — the question of how economic systems can be designed to produce better collective outcomes rather than reinforcing extraction and inequality.

Through that lens, the Anduril age is genuinely complex.

On one hand, autonomous warfare concentrates the benefits (GDP growth, technological spillover, strategic dominance) in the United States while exporting costs (forced military spending, diverted development resources) to adversaries. That's extraction at geopolitical scale.

On the other hand, it substitutes machines for human lives — including the lives of working-class Americans who have historically borne the disproportionate human cost of American wars. If you believe those lives have been spent cheaply and unjustly in the service of abstract strategic interests, then a paradigm that removes human bodies from the equation deserves serious consideration.

There's also a domestic equity dimension. Arsenal-1 is being built in Ohio — not in coastal defense corridors. Anduril's manufacturing expansion could represent a genuine reindustrialization of the American heartland, creating high-wage technical jobs in places that have been hollowed out by the offshoring of the previous industrial base. That's a collaborative economics outcome worth noting.

The Anduril age doesn't resolve the deeper question of whether American military engagement serves human flourishing. What it does is change the terms of that engagement — and in doing so, opens a new conversation about what war costs, who bears those costs, and whether the old moral calculus still applies.

## Conclusion: The Rational Paradigm

The Age of Anduril is not a choice. It's arriving regardless — driven by AI progress, by great power competition, by the structural inability of democratic societies to sustain human casualties in discretionary conflicts, and by the explicit strategy of companies like Anduril that are building its infrastructure right now, in Ohio, with venture capital and DoD contracts.

A strategically managed Anduril-era paradigm looks like this: sustained investment in autonomous systems that keeps adversaries perpetually behind the technology curve, generates continuous domestic rGDP through defense-industrial manufacturing and R&D activity, produces technological spillovers that compound civilian economic advantages, and does all of this without the political and human costs that have historically constrained American power projection.

Is it morally comfortable? No. Strategic advantage rarely is.

Is it economically coherent? Demonstrably yes.

The forever war powered by humans was unacceptable — in human cost, in political sustainability, in post-conflict economic drag. The forever war powered by robots is a different calculation entirely. The adversary pays the same tax. America doesn't pay the human price. The domestic economy grows. The technology ecosystem advances.

America's rivals are already doing that math. China's drone programs, Russia's autonomous torpedo development, South Korea's sentry guns — they're all running the same calculation from the other side of the ledger.

We should be doing it better. Anduril is already trying.
